    The ask

    Argus News is a regional Odia news channel — digital since 2018, on satellite TV since 2021 — competing against five established players for the same audience. Understand how Odia audiences actually consume news, find out where Argus stands, and define where it should stand.

    What I did

    • Sized and segmented the market — Odisha's news market by medium, geography and content format, including the Odia diaspora, which the market consistently underserves.
    • Benchmarked six channels across ten dimensions — OTV, Kanak News, Kalinga TV, News18 Odia, Nandighosha TV and Argus, on digital performance, advertiser mix, programme recall, IP events, website and Instagram presence, and engagement strategy.
    • Designed and ran the primary survey — a five-part instrument in both English and Odia, mixing multiple choice and 1–7 rating scales, fielded over six days through WhatsApp and personal networks. Responses spanned cities, towns, villages and out-of-state Odias; ages 18 to 60+; students, business owners, salaried employees, farmers and homemakers.
    • Audited the digital footprint programme by programme — YouTube performance by show and format, caption strategy, thumbnail strategy, website traffic.
    • Built the positioning — two axes I defined myself, all six competitors plotted, the empty quadrant identified, the statement written and then tested four ways: does it own a single value, is it true, is it unique, is it hard to copy.

    The perceptual map

    Positions are the study's qualitative reads on the two axes, not scored metrics.

    What came out

    • The audience had already left television. The overwhelming majority consumed news on mobile and social platforms; TV-only viewers were a small minority. For a channel that had only just arrived on satellite TV, that reframed the whole growth question.
    • The problem was reach, not quality. Argus scored well with the people who watched it and still sat third on share of viewership in both digital and TV. Satisfaction was not the constraint. Discovery was.
    • Format beat content, decisively. Short-form video massively outperformed full episodes of the same material. On one show, in the same format and the same slot, captions framed around family and emotional drama drew 258,000 views against 380 for conventional news captions. Same content. Different framing.
    • The podcast was the sleeper asset — averaging roughly nine times the views of the next-best show, and being run as a side project rather than a brand.
    “For the people of Odisha who deserve to be heard, Argus News is the only channel that fearlessly gives them a voice.”

    Seven recommendations, each tied to the evidence that produced it

    Scale the badly under-invested WhatsApp channel · build the podcast into a premium content brand · produce platform-native short-form instead of re-cutting live TV · run village-level events · create a panchayat-level youth community for news and complaints · build a CSR arm · run corporate and campus outreach.

    Scope note

    100-respondent stimulus test concentrated in one age band and skewing male — reads the campaign's likely-buyer segment well, not representative of all of India. The 27% preference is stated preference after a single exposure, not observed behaviour.

    Evidence base

    38 numbered sources, including RBI, ICRA, PwC, EY, S&P Global, TransUnion CIBIL and ICICI Securities.

    The ask

    Understand the Indian gold loan market and how borrowers behave, evaluate how the category communicates, and assess whether a running campaign was actually working.

    What I did

    • Mapped the market's structure — size, growth and drivers; the split between the organised sector and the unorganised one that still holds the majority of it; and how banks and NBFCs compete on different axes, NBFCs winning on disbursal speed and rural reach, banks on rate.
    • Worked the product mechanics — in-branch versus doorstep and digital journeys, documentation, repayment structures, tenure and loan-to-value, including a worked LTV calculation on a 50g 22-carat example and the effect of the RBI's June 2025 Lending Against Gold Collateral Directions, under which bullet-repayment LTV is computed inclusive of accrued interest at maturity.
    • Analysed how every major brand communicates, using a four-level communication framework applied across the category, plus a full teardown of Muthoot Finance's campaign portfolio — the celebrity trust platform, the 2026 outdoor campaign repositioning gold from distress to empowerment, the user-generated storytelling series and the Mahakumbh activation.
    • Ran the effectiveness study — showed 100 respondents the campaign film, then asked structured questions on recall, message clarity, emotional response, credibility and brand preference. Analysed in Excel.

    The finding, in one chart

    Message clarity
    92%
    Category awareness
    95%
    Brand familiarity
    80%
    Believability
    67%
    Brand preference
    27%
    Would not prefer
    57%
    Clarity is not preference. The campaign had solved comprehension and recall and had not touched credibility or customer benefit — the two things that actually move a borrower.

    The structural insight

    Indian households hold an enormous stock of gold and only a small single-digit share of it is pledged formally. The category's real competitor is not a rival lender's interest rate — it is the borrower's psychological barrier: stigma, fear of auction, and doubt about honest valuation. Nobody in the category was advertising against that. Spending more on the same message would have bought more of the wrong outcome.

    The ask

    Reliance Retail has been acquiring heritage Indian brands — Campa, BPL, Kelvinator — to skip the long, expensive phase of building brand equity from scratch. Does that work?

    The argument, in four moves

    • What they are actually buying is recall. The early FMCG wins came from three things together: nostalgia, aggressive pricing and deep kirana penetration. Nostalgia lowers customer acquisition cost. It does not manufacture trust.
    • The category breaks the model. A ₹10 soda is instant-gratification, low-risk, low-involvement. A ₹20,000+ appliance requires trust in the technology, the reliability and the service network. And Gen Z has no lived memory of Kelvinator or BPL, so for a large slice of the market the nostalgia asset is worth nothing at all.
    • Price-led entry is a trap. Undercutting incumbents by 20–30% buys short-term volume and buys margin compression, quality-perception damage and weak brand credibility across years three and four. Price competition creates a Red Ocean, not an advantage.
    • The alternative — “Modernised Nostalgia”. Applied the ERRC grid: eliminate the cost and time of building equity from zero; reduce middlemen markups and shelf-price inefficiency; raise distribution reach and retailer margins; create heritage trust fused with future-ready innovation. Benchmarked the Bosch Smart Security ecosystem model and proposed a Jio-powered connected-living ecosystem — appliances as nodes in an integrated smart home rather than standalone devices, backed by specialised service hubs and extended warranties as trust multipliers.
    The differentiator isn't the logo — it's the platform.

    Built and demonstrated as an academic project — not deployed into a live business, so there is no conversion data. The workflow is fully functional and importable into any n8n instance.

    The problem

    A small custom-jewellery business gets enquiries through a website form. Every enquiry looks identical in the inbox. Someone ready to buy a bridal set and someone idly asking about prices get the same delay and the same generic reply — so the ready buyer goes cold while the team works through the queue.

    What I built — click any node

    Two design decisions I focused on

    • Every branch validates before it sends. A filter checks that a usable email address exists before either Gmail node fires, so a bad submission fails silently instead of erroring the workflow.
    • The cold path still gets a reply. The easy build sends hot leads a reply and drops everyone else. But a cold lead who gets one useful, unpushy answer is a warm lead in three months. Ignoring them is how small businesses lose their own pipeline.
    The business problem was response latency and register — not response volume.

    The research problem

    GenAI tools reliably produce cleaner, faster, more polished work. The assumption is that better output means more satisfied users. Across our interviews it repeatedly did not — people reported discomfort, reduced sense of authenticity and quiet self-doubt because the output was good.

    Method

    Exploratory qualitative design — the phenomenon is too new to hypothesis-test. Nine in-depth semi-structured interviews with long-term AI users, audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim, then thematically analysed through open, axial and selective coding. Grounded in Self-Determination Theory, Identity and Authenticity theory, and the Job Demands–Resources model.

    Four themes

    • The efficiency paradox. Users produce more and feel they contribute less. The role shifts from creator to verifier.
    • Ownership erosion. The work is technically superior and emotionally unowned — a diminished sense of achievement even on better output.
    • Authenticity mismatch. The output does not sound like the person. In professional communication, where a personal register is the differentiator, that reads as generic.
    • Pragmatic adoption through human governance. The users who stayed satisfied were the ones who kept judgement in their own hands.

    The business implication

    Organisations rolling out AI on a pure-productivity business case are measuring the wrong variable. Every technical gain in our coding map paired with a psychological strain — rapid generation with cognitive overload from constant verification, polished output with loss of personal voice. The recommendation: position AI as complement not replacement, build human-in-the-loop processes where people ideate first and AI refines, invest in AI literacy, and measure process and initiative rather than only finished output.

    The ask

    Analyse Levi Strauss & Co.'s position in the Indian market — competitive landscape, external environment, internal strengths, and consumer behaviour — and propose a segmentation, targeting and positioning strategy.

    What we did

    • Mapped the competitive landscape — Levi's holds around 40–45% of India's denim segment and ~4.2% of the broader fashion market. Compared against Wrangler, Lee, H&M, Zara and other domestic players on distribution, pricing and brand equity.

    The ask

    Run an actual micro-business from scratch — not a case study, not a simulation — and apply microeconomic modelling to every decision from pricing to inventory.

    What we did

    • Launched "Chill Sip" — a premium iced tea stall on campus selling three flavours (Peach, Cranberry and Blueberry). Priced Peach at ₹40 and Cranberry/Blueberry at ₹50 based on a pre-launch survey and a per-cup cost of ₹34.
    • Collected real sales data across multiple days — tracked units sold by flavour, time slot and price point to build actual demand curves, not textbook assumptions.
    • Computed price elasticity of demand — measured how quantity responded to price changes across flavours and used the data to find the revenue-maximising price point.
    • Built total cost functions and ran break-even analysis — derived cost curves from actual operating data (ingredients, cups, utilities), fitted a polynomial cost function, and identified the break-even quantity.
    • Analysed cross-elasticity against competitors — measured how nearby alternatives (cold coffee, juice stalls) affected our demand.
    • Tested the effect of a brand ambassador on demand — ran a promotion with a campus influencer and measured the shift in the price-elasticity curve.
    Real revenue, real costs, real customers — every concept from the microeconomics course applied to data we collected ourselves.

    Why this matters

    Every company that touches customer data in India faces the same deadline. This project involved reading the regulation, translating it into business actions with owners and timelines, and thinking through second-order effects on marketing analytics — not just summarising the law.

    Concepts applied

    DPDP Act 2023Data mappingConsent designTouchpoint layersPrivacy-impact modellingPhased implementation

    The ask

    India's Digital Personal Data Protection Rules commence in May 2027. Pick a real, consumer-facing organisation and produce a practical implementation guide its management team could actually use — not an explanation of the law, but a plan: what applies, what to do about it, in what order, who owns each action, and what evidence proves it is done.

    What I did

    • Mapped eleven data entry points end to end — website checkout, mobile app, virtual try-on, beauty quiz, 100+ exclusive outlets, ~45,000 multi-brand counters, marketplaces, WhatsApp, influencer content, ad platforms and customer care — classifying each by the course's (−1)/(0)/(+1) touchpoint layers.
    • Audited every DPDP obligation against SUGAR's current state — fourteen requirements from notice and consent to breach reporting and cross-border transfers, with the gap documented row by row.
    • Made five scoping decisions that removed work — no sensitive-data category in Indian law, no legitimate-interest ground, no general data-localisation mandate, marketplace buyers are largely not our Data Principals, and the fixed erasure clock does not yet reach SUGAR. Each saved months of effort.
    • Designed twenty-nine actions across five workstreams — each with a named owner, evidence of completion, effort estimate and deadline. Workstreams: data mapping and purpose minimisation, notice and consent redesign, offline till compliance, third-party and processor contracts, and governance and customer rights.
    • Sequenced into three phases with a clear rationale: cheapest high-exposure items first (Aug–Nov 2026), the longest-pole offline and consent build second (Nov 2026–Feb 2027), and the discrete security and retention build third (Feb–May 2027).
    • Modelled the marketing-analytics impact — ran a "forty-per-cent decline" scenario showing which analytics methods survive intact (basket analysis, media mix modelling, RFM on consented base), which degrade (platform ROAS, lookalike audiences), and which practices must stop entirely (advertising to declared under-18s, buying third-party lists, blasting the legacy database on bundled consent).
    Framed compliance as a consent-design problem rather than a data-reduction problem — a well-designed consent screen leaves SUGAR with more usable first-party data than it has today.

    Why this matters

    Most analytics projects stop at reporting. This one ends in decisions — the interface tells you what to do, not just what happened. The dimensional consistency means no two screens can disagree with each other.

    System architecture

    Investment & return treesKPI registerLead vs lag KPIsDimensional taggingFact & dimension tablesDecision-first design

    The ask

    Design and build the system a real business would actually use to decide where to invest, what is working, what is not, and what to do next — not a set of charts, but a decision-making system with internally consistent investment and return trees, a KPI structure, dimensional tagging, input screens and interfaces for different decision-makers.

    What we built

    • Chose a multi-brand mobile retail business — Meher's Mobile — with multiple stores, districts, product categories and customer segments, giving enough dimensionality for the system to prove itself.
    • Built complete investment and return trees — every rupee the business spends lands on exactly one branch of the investment tree; every outcome lands on the return tree. Both carry live rupee figures computed from the same fact rows.
    • Designed a KPI centre with lead and lag separation — each KPI carries its own target, comparisons (best-so-far, previous period, target), and a verdict derived automatically. Fourteen KPIs across revenue, margin, inventory, customer acquisition and retention.
    • Built three decision views for three users — a Leadership screen for the owner (monthly, strategic: should I open another store?), an Operating screen for owner + staff (weekly, tactical: which brands to reorder, which slow stock to discount), and a Diagnostic screen (on-demand: why did margin fall — was it mix, discount or cost?).
    • Designed the input layer end to end — a Sale Entry form that writes to fact and dimension tables, with field-level validation, data types, who enters it, how often, and which tables it populates. Sixteen tables with explicit keys and relationships.
    • Delivered as a working HTML system — nine interactive screens running on synthetic data, with live filters by period, store and district. Every number on every screen traces back to the same underlying fact rows.
    Not a dashboard — a system where every screen exists to serve a named decision, and every number traces to a single source of truth.

    The ask

    Create a complete brand from the ground up — not just a name and a logo, but the full marketing architecture: positioning, product mix, SKU-level pricing, sizing strategy and a creative trigger ready for an agency briefing. Benchmarked against Marks & Spencer.

    What we built

    • Defined the brand positioning — Rare Bleu competes in the premium lifestyle segment against Marks & Spencer, Uniqlo and Zara. Positioned around "premium comfort that fits into everyday life" for urban working professionals and modern families (MHI ₹5L+, SEC A1).
    • Designed the full product mix — seven product lines (Women's Wear, Men's Wear, Innerwear & Lingerie, Kids' Wear, Footwear & Accessories, Beauty & Personal Care, Home & Living) with 110+ individually defined SKUs, an inclusive sizing strategy aligned with international and Indian standards.
    • Set SKU-level pricing — minimum and maximum price ranges for every SKU, accounting for fabric quality, size, fit, seasonal variation and promotional pricing. Ranges run from ₹499 (basics) to ₹12,999 (winter coats), consistent with the premium mass-market positioning.
    • Wrote the creative trigger — a complete agency briefing document covering competitive frame, consumer insight, target audience, advertising objective, key benefit, support points and brand mandatories.
    Not a pitch deck — a brand built with enough depth that a real business could launch from it.
